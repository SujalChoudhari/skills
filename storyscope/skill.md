# Storyscope

Use this skill to give blogs, books, essays, reviews, tutorials, technical
explanations, and other reader-facing work stronger human narrative structure.
It translates the structural findings of Russell et al., “StoryScope:
Investigating idiosyncrasies in AI fiction” (arXiv:2604.03136v6, 2026), into
editorial checks.

This is not a detector-evasion recipe. It does not try to make text score as
human, add random imperfections, or imitate a particular author's fingerprints.
It helps a real writer make choices that are concrete, varied, socially aware,
and honest about what remains unresolved.

## When to use

Use when:

- drafting or revising a blog post, book chapter, essay, review, tutorial,
  technical explanation, or other prose meant for people to read;
- the sentences are clean but the piece still feels assembled, over-explained,
  generic, or too neatly resolved;
- the user asks for writing that feels more human at the level of structure,
  not only vocabulary or punctuation;
- a long piece needs cases, anecdotes, counterpressure, pacing, or a stronger
  relationship between evidence and conclusion.

Do not use this skill to:

- invent anecdotes, witnesses, quotations, sources, memories, or first-person
  experience;
- force flashbacks, subplots, direct reader address, moral ambiguity, or a
  messy ending into a piece that does not earn them;
- claim that a draft is human-written, undetectable, or safe because it matches
  a checklist;
- replace fact-checking, privacy review, continuity/canon review, or the user's
  own voice and final creative decisions.

## Evidence boundary

The paper studies fiction, not blogs or books in general. The authors create a
parallel corpus of 10,272 human-written stories and stories generated by five
LLMs from the same prompts, yielding 61,608 stories and 304 interpretable
narrative features across ten dimensions: Agent, Social Network, Event, Plot,
Structure, Setting, Time, Revelation, Perspective, and Style.

In that controlled corpus, narrative features without the style dimension
reached 93.2% macro-F1 for human-versus-AI detection and 68.4% macro-F1 for
six-way source attribution. After a separate surface-style editing procedure,
narrative detection remained high. The authors report that AI stories more often
made themes explicit, used tidy single-track causality, supplied embodied or
sensory explanation, and resolved tension through internal understanding.
Human stories in this corpus showed more structural variety, named or specific
intertextual references, dialogue and social-world involvement, temporal
complexity, subplots, morally mixed protagonists, and room for reader inference.

These are group-level findings from a particular fiction corpus and pipeline.
The human stories come from a Books3-derived research dataset, not a
representative sample of every kind of human writing, and the paper does not
release those human stories because of copyright concerns. Treat the findings as
editorial hypotheses, not universal laws, proof of quality, or instructions to
invert every AI-leaning feature. The classifier is not an editor and its
percentages are not a target for a writer.

Source: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03136> and
<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.03136>.

## Structural map

Before drafting or revising a long piece, make a small internal map. Do not show
it to the reader unless the user asks for an outline.

- **Anchor:** What concrete situation, object, case, scene, or observed detail
  gives the piece a body?
- **Agents:** Who acts, wants, decides, resists, notices, or bears the cost?
- **Pressure:** What constraint, tradeoff, uncertainty, or competing interest
  makes the piece move?
- **Events:** What actually happens, in a causal sequence the reader can follow?
- **Agency:** Which outcome comes from a person's choice, and which comes from
  circumstance, another person, a system, or chance?
- **Social world:** Who else matters? Whose view, need, or resistance changes the
  meaning of the central subject?
- **Revelation:** What does the reader learn, when do they learn it, and does the
  new information genuinely change an earlier interpretation?
- **Time:** Is a linear order clearest, or would a deliberate time shift earn its
  place by delaying or revising understanding?
- **Texture:** Which concrete places, objects, named references, dialogue, or
  technical details make the subject specific?
- **Remainder:** What cannot honestly be solved, proved, or tied up by the end?

A short post may need only an anchor, pressure, evidence, and consequence. A
chapter may need the full map. Never fill empty slots with inventions.

## Procedure

### 1. Bind the real artifact and evidence

Read the brief, source material, existing draft, and relevant voice or canon
before proposing structure. Separate three layers:

1. **Source fact:** what a paper, person, repository, experiment, or document
   actually supports;
2. **Author observation:** what the writer has personally seen or done and is
   allowed to claim;
3. **Editorial synthesis:** the judgment or interpretation the piece is making.

For public work, use only public or explicitly approved facts. An anecdote is
allowed only when it comes from supplied or verified experience. A composite or
hypothetical example must be labelled when that distinction matters. Never add a
named colleague, customer, meeting, failure, book, quote, or tool because a
paragraph needs a more vivid human detail.

Completion check: every proposed scene, example, statistic, and first-person
claim has a known source, an explicit hypothetical label, or is removed.

When a legitimate anecdote belongs in the piece, shape it as concrete detail,
action, friction, consequence, and limited reflection. The reflection should
narrow the meaning rather than announce a universal lesson. If removing the
anecdote leaves the argument unchanged, it was decoration and should be cut.

### 2. Choose the shape before polishing sentences

Pick the structure that serves the material. Useful shapes include:

- **Case to consequence:** a concrete situation, pressure, what happened, what
  changed, and what the author now thinks;
- **Question to complication:** a clear question, the obvious answer, evidence
  that makes the answer less complete, and a narrower conclusion;
- **Object to world:** one object, tool, line of code, room, or ritual opening
  outward into people, history, systems, and stakes;
- **Chronological investigation:** events in order when the order itself teaches
  the reader how the result emerged;
- **Recontextualized sequence:** begin with a consequence or partial view, then
  move through anchored earlier events so a later fact changes interpretation;
- **Braided chapter:** two or more threads that meet through a real shared
  pressure, not parallel examples pasted together for variety.

Do not use the same shape for every post or chapter. Do not make a piece
nonlinear merely to look literary. If a time jump does not change meaning, cut
it. If a subplot does not create contrast, pressure, or necessary context, cut
it.

Completion check: the chosen shape can be stated in one plain sentence, and
every major section has a reason to exist beyond “the outline needed another
section.”

### 3. Draft with human structural choices

Use the paper's dimensions as questions, not requirements.

#### Agents and agency

- Give the central person, team, system, or object a specific role in the event.
- Show what they choose and what they cannot control.
- Do not make every resolution arrive through insight, acceptance, or a neat
  protagonist decision. Keep external constraints visible when they matter.
- In technical prose, an agent may be a maintainer, user, API, queue, compiler,
  failure, or organizational rule. Do not personify a system unless the voice
  clearly calls for it.

#### Social world and outside pressure

- Let the subject meet another perspective, stakeholder, source, tradition,
  constraint, or consequence when the material supports one.
- Use real named works, places, standards, tools, or people when specificity
  earns its place. Verify them. Avoid vague allusions and impressive-sounding
  name drops.
- Direct reader address is optional and genre-bound. Do not sprinkle “you”
  through a piece as a shortcut to intimacy.

#### Events, plot, and subplots

- Replace feature inventory with movement: a decision, failure, discovery,
  workaround, cost, or consequence.
- Let a second thread disagree with or complicate the main argument when it is
  real. Do not manufacture side stories to avoid a tidy structure.
- Keep causal links honest. Use “because” only when evidence supports cause;
  otherwise use “after,” “alongside,” or “we do not know.”

#### Theme and moralizing

- Let an example, scene, or result carry some of the meaning.
- Before writing an explicit lesson, ask whether the reader can already infer
  it. Cut explanatory sentences that merely repeat what the piece has shown.
- Keep a conclusion when the reader needs one, but make it narrower than the
  evidence warrants rather than grander.

#### Time and revelation

- Keep chronology when it reduces confusion or is part of the teaching.
- Use a flashback, delayed fact, or frame only when it reveals a real causal or
  emotional relationship.
- Anchor every jump with a clear time signal. A reveal should recontextualize
  earlier material, not merely withhold information for suspense.
- Preserve uncertainty when the source or event does not resolve it. Do not add
  a twist to make ordinary evidence feel like a story.

#### Emotion, setting, and embodiment

- Do not rely on a default catalogue of tightened chests, cold sweat, dim light,
  and other body or sensory metaphors. Use behavior, dialogue, omission, choice,
  timing, and concrete objects as well.
- Keep sensory detail when it carries information or voice. The rule is not
  “less sensory writing”; it is “do not use sensory description as a substitute
  for observation.”
- A setting should be more than a mood mirror when the material allows it. Let a
  place, tool, institution, or physical limit affect what can happen.

#### Narrative diversity

- Vary the scale of sections, distance from the subject, type of evidence, and
  rhythm of explanation when the piece is long enough to need it.
- Seek one truthful detail or angle specific to this subject rather than adding
  novelty at random.
- Do not mistake disorder for humanity. A clear linear tutorial, restrained
  review, or simple ending can be exactly right.

Completion check: the draft contains at least one concrete movement from
pressure to consequence, and no structural device exists only because a
checklist asked for it.

### 4. Adapt the pass to the artifact

**Blog or essay:** A reliable default is concrete opening, actual problem or
question, evidence or case, complication, author judgment, and a consequence
smaller and more honest than a generic moral. Let sources support the argument
without turning the post into a research-paper scaffold. State what the author
thinks and what the source merely reports.

**Technical book or chapter:** Use a real case, implementation detail,
experiment, failure, or reader problem as the centre. Give enough mechanism to
understand the choice, then show a tradeoff, edge case, or limitation. Vary
chapter movement without breaking chronology, terminology, or canon. Keep
prototype behaviour, verified behaviour, proposed fixes, and open questions
distinct.

**Documentation or tutorial:** Put the reader in a real task, show the smallest
working path, explain why it works, then include one failure mode or boundary.
Do not invent a user story when a direct example is clearer.

**Fiction:** Follow the project's canon, voice, continuity, and ownership rules
first. Use Storyscope as a diagnostic for agency, social pressure, revelation,
time, and subtext. Do not compile missing details into canon.

Completion check: the artifact's required clarity, continuity, and factual gates
still pass after the structural pass.

### 5. Run language cleanup separately

Storyscope is mainly about narrative content and structure. Keep surface-writing
requirements as a separate pass:

- use simple English and active verbs;
- vary sentence length without artificial fragments everywhere;
- use ordinary punctuation, including ASCII punctuation when the project style
  requires it;
- remove filler, promotional language, vague authorities, generic conclusions,
  and repeated signposting;
- keep real humour, opinion, hesitation, and rough edges when they belong to the
  writer;
- do not insert typos, awkwardness, fake slang, or random “human” mistakes;
- do not add emojis or decorative formatting to serious prose unless requested.

Read the result aloud. If every paragraph lands with the same polished rhythm,
opens with the same abstract claim, and closes with the same lesson, revise the
structure rather than only changing synonyms.

Completion check: the prose is simpler and more specific, but its meaning,
claims, examples, and author voice have not been invented or flattened.

## Final review questions

1. What is the concrete anchor, and does it appear early enough?
2. Who or what acts, and what remains outside its control?
3. Where does the piece encounter another person, source, constraint, or world?
4. Did any paragraph merely state the theme already demonstrated?
5. Are chronology, withheld facts, and reveals helping understanding or performing
   complexity?
6. Is every anecdote real, supplied, verified, or clearly hypothetical?
7. Did the piece preserve true uncertainty instead of manufacturing resolution?
8. Does each section add a new fact, scene, judgment, or consequence?
9. Are specific references real and relevant rather than vague decoration?
10. Does the ending leave an earned consequence or judgment rather than a generic
    “this shows why it matters” paragraph?

Then run the artifact's normal fact, privacy, citation, canon, code, and build
checks. This skill never turns a structural review into publication approval.

## Pitfalls

- **Feature inversion:** A corpus tendency does not mean every human piece needs
  a flashback or subplot.
- **Checklist prose:** Adding one anecdote, subplot, and direct address by
  formula creates another template.
- **Fake life:** A vivid invented incident is still a fabricated claim when
  presented as the author's experience.
- **Ambiguity theatre:** Vagueness is not depth when the facts support a clear
  answer.
- **Novelty theatre:** Random references and unusual chronology do not create
  originality by themselves.
- **Detector chasing:** Structural improvement does not make authorship
  classification reliable. Never promise it.
- **Source laundering:** A paper's classifier output does not validate a blog's
  anecdote, technical claim, or biography.
- **Overcorrection:** Do not remove every clear thesis, linear explanation, or
  sensory detail. Reader trust also comes from clarity.

## Verification

A successful use leaves an inspectable editorial decision:

- the structural map is coherent;
- examples and anecdotes have an evidence status;
- source fact, author observation, and synthesis are not blended accidentally;
- the draft has no forced structural devices or generic moral padding;
- chronology, continuity, terminology, and reader clarity survive;
- public content passes factual and privacy boundaries;
- the final artifact passes ordinary build or rendering checks when relevant;
- no claim is made that the writing is “human” because a classifier-like pattern
  was avoided.
