# Storyscope

Use Storyscope when drafting or revising a blog post, essay, book chapter, review,
tutorial, technical explanation, or fiction draft that feels generic, assembled,
over-explained, or too neatly resolved.

Storyscope is an editorial procedure, not an AI detector or detector-evasion
recipe. It improves specificity, agency, pressure, social context, revelation,
and earned conclusions without inventing experience or flattening the writer's
voice.

## Leading words

Use these words as a compact map while working:

- **Anchor:** the concrete situation, object, case, scene, or observed detail that
  gives the piece a body.
- **Pressure:** the constraint, tradeoff, uncertainty, or competing interest that
  makes the piece move.
- **Agency:** what a person, team, system, or circumstance chooses or controls.
- **Consequence:** what changes, costs something, or becomes newly understood.
- **Remainder:** what the evidence, event, or ending cannot honestly resolve.

## When to use

Use it when:

- the user wants structural revision rather than synonym-level polishing;
- a long piece needs stronger cases, pacing, social context, or counterpressure;
- an explanation states its lesson repeatedly instead of letting evidence carry it;
- a draft needs a more specific relationship between claims, examples, and readers.

Keep the normal editorial process when the piece is already structurally fit. Do not
use Storyscope to add fake anecdotes, first-person experience, quotations, sources,
flashbacks, subplots, moral ambiguity, or an intentionally messy ending.

## Procedure

### 1. Bind the artifact

Read the brief, current draft, supplied sources, and any approved voice/canon
material before proposing structure. Label each important element as one of:

- **Source fact:** supported by a paper, document, repository, experiment, or other
  cited source.
- **Author observation:** supplied or verified experience the writer is allowed to
  claim.
- **Editorial synthesis:** an interpretation or judgment made by the piece.

A hypothetical or composite example is labelled when the distinction matters.

**Done when:** every proposed example, statistic, scene, and first-person claim has
a source, an explicit hypothetical status, or has been removed.

### 2. Make a structural map

Write a private map before sentence polishing:

```text
Anchor:
Agents and agency:
Pressure:
Events and causal movement:
Consequence:
Remainder or open question:
```

For a long piece, also record social world, revelation, time order, and texture
(concrete places, objects, named references, dialogue, or technical detail).

A short post may need only anchor, pressure, evidence, and consequence. Do not fill
empty fields merely to complete the map.

**Done when:** the map identifies one real movement from pressure to consequence and
names what remains uncertain.

### 3. Choose the shape

Choose the structure that the material earns. Examples include:

- case to consequence;
- question to complication;
- object to world;
- chronological investigation;
- recontextualized sequence;
- braided chapter where threads meet through shared pressure.

State the chosen shape in one plain sentence. Keep linear structure when it teaches
most clearly. Use a time jump only when it changes interpretation; use a subplot only
when it adds real pressure, contrast, or context.

**Done when:** every major section has a reason beyond satisfying an outline slot.

### 4. Revise structural causes, not surface symptoms

Work through the map in this order:

1. Replace feature inventory with decisions, failures, discoveries, workarounds,
   costs, and consequences.
2. Show what the central person, team, system, or object controls and what remains
   outside its control.
3. Add another perspective, stakeholder, source, tradition, or constraint only when
   the material supplies one.
4. Let examples, scenes, and results carry meaning before adding an explicit lesson.
5. Keep causal language precise: use “because” only when evidence supports cause;
   otherwise use “after,” “alongside,” or “we do not know.”
6. Preserve unresolved evidence instead of manufacturing a twist or resolution.

**Done when:** the revision contains no structural device added solely because a
checklist requested it.

### 5. Adapt to the artifact

- **Blog or essay:** concrete opening, actual problem, evidence or case,
  complication, author judgment, and a narrower consequence.
- **Technical writing:** a real reader task, smallest working path, mechanism,
  tradeoff or failure mode, and explicit boundary between verified behavior,
  proposed behavior, and open questions.
- **Fiction:** canon, continuity, voice, ownership, agency, social pressure,
  revelation, time, and subtext remain authoritative.
- **Documentation:** prefer a direct example when a manufactured user story adds
  nothing.

Read `references/editorial-checklist.md` when the artifact needs the extended
shape and final-review questions.

### 6. Run a separate language pass

After structural work, simplify the prose without changing its claims or voice:
remove filler, promotional language, vague authorities, repeated signposting, and
generic moral padding. Vary sentence length naturally. Keep useful hesitation,
humor, opinion, and roughness. Never add typos or awkwardness as proof of humanity.

**Done when:** the prose is more specific and readable, while its evidence, meaning,
examples, terminology, continuity, and voice remain intact.

### 7. Verify the handoff

Return:

- the chosen shape and one-sentence rationale;
- the anchor, pressure, agency, consequence, and remainder;
- the evidence status of examples and first-person claims;
- structural changes made and changes deliberately rejected;
- unresolved questions and the ordinary fact, privacy, citation, canon, code, or
  rendering checks still required.

Read `references/research-boundary.md` when explaining the StoryScope research.
It records the paper's scope and prevents fiction-corpus findings from being
presented as universal rules for all writing.

A successful pass improves reader trust and structural specificity. It does not
claim that the result is human-written, undetectable, or publication-ready merely
because a checklist was completed.

## Pitfalls

- **Feature inversion:** a corpus tendency does not mean every human piece needs a
  flashback, subplot, dialogue, or ambiguity.
- **Checklist prose:** adding one anecdote, subplot, and direct address by formula
  creates another template.
- **Fake life:** an invented incident remains fabricated when presented as experience.
- **Ambiguity theatre:** vagueness is not depth when the facts support a clear answer.
- **Source laundering:** a research classifier does not validate an anecdote,
  technical claim, or biography.
- **Overcorrection:** clear thesis, linear explanation, and sensory detail can be
  exactly right.
